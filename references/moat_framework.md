# Moat Framework

A moat is a structural reason a company can earn returns above its cost of capital for years without
being competed away. Don't assert a moat — test for it and score its durability.

## The moat types (test each)

1. **Switching costs** — customers lose money/time/risk by leaving. Test: contract length, retention
   rate, integration depth, retraining cost. Strong in enterprise software, payment rails, banks.
2. **Network effects** — each user makes the product more valuable to others. Test: does value scale
   with the network? Is it local or global? Multi-homing easy or hard? Strong in marketplaces,
   exchanges, social, payments.
3. **Intangible assets** — brand, patents, licenses, regulatory approvals. Test: can the company
   charge more for an identical product? Is the patent/licence cliff near? Strong in luxury, pharma,
   regulated utilities.
4. **Cost advantage** — structurally lower cost (scale, process, location, unique asset). Test:
   position on the cost curve; can rivals replicate it? Strong in commodities, low-cost retail,
   foundries with process leadership.
5. **Efficient scale** — a market only big enough to profitably support one or a few players, so
   entry is irrational. Test: market size vs minimum efficient scale. Strong in pipelines, regional
   utilities, niche industrials.
6. **Cornered resource / learning curve** — exclusive access to an input, or a yield/process lead
   that compounds with volume (relevant to semis: learning-curve cost declines).

## Durability scoring (do this explicitly)

For the identified moat(s), score:
- **Width** — how much excess return does it protect? (margin/ROIC premium vs peers)
- **Direction** — is the moat widening, stable, or eroding? Direction matters more than width.
- **Time** — roughly how many years until a credible threat (tech shift, patent cliff, regulation,
  new entrant)?

A wide-but-eroding moat can be worth less than a narrow-but-widening one. Always state the trend.

## Common errors to avoid
- Confusing a *good product* or *current market share* with a moat. A lead competitors can buy or
  out-spend is not a moat.
- Confusing growth with quality. Fast growth into a contestable market invites competition.
- Ignoring the moat's *cost*: heavy reinvestment to maintain a moat lowers free cash flow.

## Tie to niche dominance (Stage 4 of the skill)
- Dominance = high + durable share *because of* one of the above mechanisms.
- Influence/chain effects = whether the company's moat lets it set terms that ripple through
  suppliers, customers, complementors, or the sector index. A true niche dominator changes others'
  economics when it moves price, capacity, or strategy.
